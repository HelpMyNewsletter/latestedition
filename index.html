Field Notes / August 18, 2026

Your newsletter isn't just a newsletter anymore.

Something's been bothering me about the way we talk about newsletter growth.

We talk about audience. Opens. Clicks. Subject lines. Content. All useful. All worth caring about.

But eventually, publishing the email becomes the easy part.

Your archive gets big enough that nobody wants to touch it. You launch Premium. Then a product. Then another product. One kind of customer should get everything. Another should get one thing. A contributor sends you a buyer and somebody needs to know who gets credit. Your team starts keeping notes in three different places because nobody's completely sure which system knows the truth.

The problem has moved.

You're running operations inside a newsletter business now.

That's the awkward middle stage: the newsletter still looks simple from the outside while the business behind it has quietly become a pile of permissions, workflows, products, people and edge cases.

Where I've Been Lately

Three problems at Hotels Above Par turned into one bigger lesson.

One of the places I've been spending a frankly absurd amount of time lately is Hotels Above Par.

Over roughly the last month, we've built three connected systems around their Beehiiv operation:

1. A content migration workflow that can rebuild older editorial pieces in the current design as they're brought over.

2. A verification and delivery layer for premium digital Boutique Books, where different readers can have different access.

3. An internal contributor referral system that can connect promotion back to attributable revenue.

I'm happy to talk about the problems those systems solved. I'm not going to publish the code, access rules, attribution logic or the client's internal plumbing. You don't need the blueprints to steal the useful part anyway.

THE OPERATING FLOW

CONTENT → PRODUCT → ACCESS → PROMOTION → ATTRIBUTION → REVENUE

Once those pieces started connecting, the pattern got very obvious: growing newsletters don't just need more content. They need systems that can keep up with what the business is becoming.

SYSTEM FAILURE 01 / THE ARCHIVE

“We already made all this content. Why are we rebuilding it by hand?”

Years of content don't magically become an asset just because they exist.

HAP had a large legacy archive with the normal scars that accumulate over time: different layouts, different image placements, different heading structures, captions, addresses, listings and formatting conventions.

The obvious job was moving the archive.

The useful job was making the archive arrive cleaner than it left.

So the migration workflow learned to recognize the parts of a piece and rebuild them in the current publishing structure. The editorial copy stays intact. The presentation gets brought forward. When a weird edge case shows up, the workflow gets smarter instead of sending the whole project back to manual production.

For publishers: migration can be a redesign opportunity. Your archive doesn't have to be a museum of every formatting decision you made in 2022.

Maybe your version is:

• 300 old posts nobody can browse.

• Years of inconsistent thumbnails and tags.

• A migration from another platform that still looks like the old platform.

• Evergreen work you paid to create that readers almost never find.

SYSTEM FAILURE 02 / ACCESS

“Okay, this person paid. What exactly do they own?”

Paid gets weird faster than people expect.

One reader gets broad access because they're Premium. Another bought one individual product. A Founder has a different entitlement. Somebody else is a perfectly valid subscriber who doesn't own any of the premium products at all.

For HAP's Boutique Books, the system needed to answer one question every time somebody showed up:

WHAT SHOULD THIS PERSON BE ABLE TO ACCESS RIGHT NOW?

Beehiiv could stay at the center as the subscriber source of truth. A lightweight verification layer checks the reader's state and sends them into the right experience.

That's when monetization starts carrying a permissions problem along with it.

And that's why “we'll just email them the download link” works beautifully right up until it absolutely doesn't.

SYSTEM FAILURE 03 / ATTRIBUTION

“One of our writers generated this sale. Can we prove it?”

Contributors have audiences. Great. Then one of those people becomes a customer and suddenly “share this with your followers” needs grown-up accounting attached to it.

Before we went shopping for affiliate software, we worked through the business rules.

Who needs a unique identity?

Which purchases count?

How do subscriptions and individual products get attributed?

How does the internal team reconcile what happened?

What does an author actually need to understand before we hand them a link?

For the initial group, a lean internal system made more sense than buying a platform designed for a much bigger affiliate operation. We can always graduate later if the volume earns the complexity.

The useful question comes before the software search: what does the business actually need to know, decide or prove?

THE SOFTWARE TRAP

Please stop making the software decision first.

Publishers are trained to solve nouns with subscriptions.

Need affiliates? Buy affiliate software. Need gated content? Buy a membership platform. Need an automation? Add another automation tool. Need a prettier site? Add another site.

Every new tab feels like progress until you're the person reconciling seven tabs on Friday afternoon.

“I NEED AFFILIATE SOFTWARE.”

What exactly has to be attributed, over what period, and how will we reconcile it?

“I NEED A MEMBERSHIP PLATFORM.”

How many meaningful access states do we actually have, and where does that truth live?

“I NEED TO MIGRATE THE ARCHIVE.”

What should the archive look like when we're finished, and what can we improve while it moves?

“I NEED ANOTHER AUTOMATION.”

Which reader state are we trying to change, and what should happen when the automation works?

“BEEHIIV CAN'T DO THIS.”

Does Beehiiv need to own the entire workflow, or can it remain the source of truth while a small layer handles the odd part?

Sometimes the correct answer really is another platform. I like software. I am not anti-tab. I just want the tab to earn its place.

THE AI PART

AI can build faster than you can think. That's useful. It's also a little dangerous.

Last week I gave you 100 prompts for asking a Beehiiv MCP to inspect your newsletter.

Here's the next thing I've learned from living inside these systems: once AI can touch the machinery, bad architecture gets faster too.

AI-assisted development helped us move quickly on the HAP work. Code could be generated, changed, tested and iterated at a pace that would've sounded ridiculous a few years ago.

The bottleneck kept landing on judgment.

Which system should remain the source of truth?

Which subscriber states actually matter?

What should happen when verification fails?

Which edge cases deserve real transaction tests?

When is the boring solution better than the clever one?

Code is getting cheaper. Knowing what deserves to exist is getting more valuable.

YOUR SYSTEMS CHECK

Has your newsletter quietly become a systems business?

Count how many of these make you wince:

01. Somebody on your team manually decides who gets access to what.

02. Your subscriber tags or custom fields are doing actual business logic now.

03. You sell more than one kind of thing.

04. Contributors, partners or affiliates influence acquisition or revenue.

05. Your archive is big enough that maintaining it has become a recurring job.

06. People copy the same information between tools because the tools don't share it cleanly.

07. One reader can exist in several meaningful states that change what should happen next.

08. You've added software to solve several tiny pieces of what is really one workflow.

THREE OR MORE?

Don't buy anything this afternoon. Map the flow first. What enters? What changes? Where does the truth live? Who gets what? What has to happen automatically? Where do you still want a human making the call?
