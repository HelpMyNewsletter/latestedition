HELP MY NEWSLETTER / NOVEMBER 17, 2026

THE SUBSCRIPTION BACKLASH

Your readers aren’t hostages.

You finally pay off the car. The payment disappears. Then you find out that a feature you use still comes with a monthly bill.

I understand why that gets under people’s skin. At some point, you want to be done paying for the thing you already bought.

Now put that feeling next to your newsletter. The promised download. The surprise login. The upgrade that leads to another upgrade. The cancellation button nobody can find.

We can build our own version of that frustration without ever meaning to.

Today’s triage: Follow one promise from your signup page to delivery. Count every extra click, login, sales pitch, and payment request between the reader and what you offered.

01 / The bill keeps finding you

The details matter here. Owning a car and subscribing to connected services are separate things. A subscription expiring doesn’t mean the whole car stops working.

But it can change which features you can use. Toyota currently lists Remote Connect at $8 a month for features such as starting or locking a compatible vehicle through a smart device. Availability and terms vary by vehicle.

Housing has its own version. Invitation Homes lists a required smart-home subscription alongside rent: $30 a month, or $40 for homes with a video doorbell, with location differences. For renters, that means another required monthly cost on top of the rent they budgeted for.

The problem gets sharper when the full price shows up late. The FTC says it is returning more than $47.2 million to eligible Invitation Homes consumers over undisclosed fees and other deceptive or unfair charges, including charges for smart-home technology and utility management.

For a newsletter publisher, that’s the warning: the customer remembers the deal they thought they were getting. Your fine print doesn’t automatically repair a broken expectation.

02 / The rebellion might look boring

I expect the pushback to get louder. I also expect a lot of it to happen without an announcement.

People choose the simpler product. Skip the upgrade. Cancel the service they can live without. Tell a friend which company finally pushed its luck.

There’s a signal worth watching: S&P Global Mobility’s 2025 Connected Car Study reported that respondents willing to pay for connected services fell from 86% in 2024 to 68% in 2025. That’s a shift in survey responses, not proof that subscriptions are finished.

My bet is that clear pricing and customer control will become stronger reasons to choose a business.

A car owner may feel stuck. Moving house can be expensive and exhausting. Your newsletter is much easier to leave.

A reader doesn’t have to cancel to be gone. They can stay on your list and stop giving you any attention at all.

That’s why I’d look beyond the subscriber count. Are people using what they bought? Are they returning, replying, and recommending you? Or are you counting people who have mentally checked out?

03 / Charge for work readers can recognize

Good publishing deserves to get paid. Reporting takes time. Research needs updating. Useful analysis requires judgment. You don’t need to apologize for charging for that work.

Recurring revenue needs a recurring reason. Tell readers what they’ll receive, how often it arrives, and what access depends on keeping the subscription.

A finished guide may make sense as a one-time purchase. A research service you update every week may make sense as a membership. If archive access ends when membership ends, explain that before checkout.

Then check the little things that can sour the relationship:

  • The free edition that never finishes a thought. Give it a complete job. Paid readers can get deeper reporting, tools, or access; free readers should still receive what you promised them.

  • The download with another door behind it. If someone signed up for a checklist, deliver the checklist. Put the optional offer after it.

  • The upgrade that keeps moving. Explain the difference between tiers before purchase. Give existing customers notice when the deal changes, and honor the commitments you made.

  • The exit that feels like a punishment. Make cancellation easy to find. Confirm when billing stops and which access remains. Treat leaving readers like people you’d welcome back.

You can change your business model. You can charge more. You can introduce a paid edition. Just make the new deal clear enough that someone can choose it without needing to decode it.

04 / Run the five-minute freedom check

Use a test account you control. Walk through one reader journey on your phone:

  1. Minute one: Read the promise. What would a reasonable person expect from this signup, purchase, or upgrade? Write that down.

  2. Minute two: Follow the path. Count the logins, clicks, pitches, and payment requests before the promised thing arrives. Keep steps that protect the account or obtain meaningful consent; question the rest.

  3. Minute three: Check the deal. Can you find the full price, billing frequency, renewal terms, and what’s included before committing?
