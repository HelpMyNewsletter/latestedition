HELP MY NEWSLETTER / NOVEMBER 10, 2026

THE READABILITY CHECK

Your newsletter looks great.
Can people actually read it?

You can spend an hour choosing a header and never spend ten seconds checking whether someone can read the offer underneath it.

We polish. We preview. We send. Then we wonder why people did not click.

Sometimes the copy needs work. Sometimes the reader never got a fair shot at it.

Tiny type. Pale links. A beautiful graphic carrying every useful detail. A button that makes perfect sense to you because you already know where it goes.

Today’s triage: Open your last sent issue on your phone. Could a reader with images blocked, larger text, or a screen reader still find the point and take the next step?

That is the test. Let’s make it easier to pass.

01 / When the pictures disappear

Your thumbnail should earn attention. It should not be the only place your message lives.

If the event date, price, offer, or call to action exists only inside a graphic, move that information into real text below it. Then give readers a real text link or button.

Alt text is the backup description. It is not a place to dump keywords or repeat an entire sales page.

Try this: Instead of “image_04.png,” describe what the reader needs: “Three steps: choose a topic, draft the issue, schedule the send.” If a chart carries the argument, put its main finding in the body too.

For a purely decorative image, an empty alt attribute lets assistive technology skip it. For an image that acts as a link, describe its purpose. W3C’s image guide shows the difference.

The check: Block images in a test email. Can you still tell what it is about, why it matters, and what to do?

02 / Stop making people squint

Muted gray on soft gray can look lovely in a mockup. It can also disappear in an inbox.

Check the actual text color against its background. WCAG’s contrast benchmark is at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. For a simple working rule, aim for 4.5:1 throughout your reading copy.

Start body copy at 17 or 18 pixels and give the lines room to breathe. That is my practical starting point, not a universal compliance rule. Read your actual send on a small screen before deciding it works.

Keep your personality. We have our own font family here, and I love a strong headline. But personality has a job: help readers recognize you. It should not make them work harder to understand you.

Use display fonts for short accents. Let your reading font carry the paragraphs. Keep a sensible fallback because email apps do not all support the same fonts.

A QUICK BEFORE / AFTER

Before: A flyer image contains the workshop name, time, and signup instructions. Beneath it: “Click here.”

After: A short heading says “Build your welcome email.” The time and details are written underneath. The link says “Reserve a workshop seat.”

Same offer. Much less guesswork.

03 / Give every link a destination

Read only the linked words in your last issue. Do they tell a story, or do they say “here,” “here,” “learn more,” and “this”?

Some screen reader users navigate by links. Other readers skim. Both deserve to know where a click will take them.

Make the label do some work: “Download the checklist.” “Compare the plans.” “Read the image-rights guide.”

Underline links so color is not the only clue. Give buttons enough space that a thumb can choose one without catching its neighbor. And use real headings in a sensible order, not bold paragraphs pretending to be headings.

W3C’s writing tips cover meaningful links and headings.

04 / Test the inbox, not just the editor

Your draft preview is useful. Your reader’s inbox gets the final vote.

Send yourself a test. Open it on your phone. Switch to dark mode. Enlarge the text. If you can, also listen with VoiceOver or TalkBack. Follow the main link all the way to its landing page.

Look for copy that vanishes, columns that squeeze, buttons that lose their labels, or a reading order that stops making sense.

beehiiv explains why emails render differently across inboxes, including blocked images, font fallbacks, and dark mode. This is why checking the actual test email matters.

YOUR FIVE-MINUTE TRIAGE

  1. One minute: Open the last issue on your phone.

  2. One minute: Block the images. Find the offer.

  3. One minute: Enlarge the text. Look for clipping.

  4. One minute: Read only the headings and link labels.

  5. One minute: Fix the biggest obstacle in your next draft.

This is a quick diagnostic, not a full accessibility audit. The point is to stop sending the same avoidable obstacle every week.

Your reader already gave you permission to show up. Make it easy for them to stay.
