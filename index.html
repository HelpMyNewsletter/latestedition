Field Notes / August 12, 2026

I disappeared for two weeks. That was not the plan.

Okay. I owe you an email.

Actually, I probably owe you two.

I haven’t sent a personal Help My Newsletter email in two weeks, and there isn’t some clever content-strategy explanation hiding behind that. I got busy. Then I got busier. Then I did the thing I tell newsletter publishers not to do: I kept taking care of everybody else’s newsletter and putting my own at the bottom of the list.

Which is...not ideal when your business is literally called Help My Newsletter.

So, no. This wasn’t a scarcity play. I wasn’t “creating anticipation.” I didn’t disappear to make you miss me.

I just flat-out slacked on my own send schedule.

And yes, I know. There is a special kind of ridiculousness in being the newsletter person who hasn’t sent her newsletter. We are simply going to acknowledge that and keep moving.

Where I Actually Went

Down the beehiiv rabbit hole. Deep.

The good news is I haven’t exactly been sitting around eating bonbons.

I’ve spent the last couple of weeks buried in the kind of client work I love most: the messy, complicated, “okay, but what is this system actually doing?” stuff.

We’ve been cleaning and restructuring a huge hotel-review archive, tightening taxonomy, checking booking links, auditing images, building better search and discovery, working through premium access and metering, and figuring out what readers actually do when they hit a gate instead of guessing.

On another publication, we’ve been rebuilding re-engagement emails with truly awful open rates, tightening voting funnels, improving participation language, and connecting a separate industry resource back into the newsletter’s subscriber-growth engine.

And we’ve been building an entirely new financial-media brand from the dirt up: positioning, newsletter, website, YouTube, conversion paths, visual systems, editorial structure, all of it.

Then there’s the less glamorous stuff, which is usually where the real work is.

Wrong tags. Broken HTML. Duplicate images. Weird automation logic. Links that technically work but don’t do what everybody thought they did. Subscriber journeys that look perfectly reasonable until you actually walk through them.

The recurring lesson: the newsletter usually doesn’t need more ideas. It needs somebody to look under the hood and figure out what the existing machinery is doing.

This Is The Part I’m Obsessed With

AI gets a LOT more useful when it can actually see the newsletter.

For years, “AI for newsletters” mostly meant copying a bunch of information out of one tool, pasting it into another tool, asking a question, getting an answer, and then going back to do the actual work yourself.

That can still be useful.

But MCP changes the shape of the job.

With the right beehiiv MCP connection, I can ask AI to inspect the publication itself. Posts. Links. Subscriber behavior. Automations. Journeys. Forms. Tags. Surveys. Signup flows. Products. Orders. Paid tiers. Website pages. Paywalls. Sponsorships. Templates.

And, where the action is supported and appropriate, it can help build or change things too.

That’s a completely different animal.

The shift: instead of pasting a screenshot and asking, “What do you think?”, you can increasingly say, “Go look. Tell me what’s actually happening.”

Before You Go Wild

My favorite sentence to add before an MCP starts touching anything

I use variations of this constantly, especially when I’m working inside a client account:

Read the existing publication data first. Don’t make changes yet. Tell me what you found, what you recommend changing, why, and what the consequences could be. Wait for my approval before executing anything.

Analysis first. Surgery second. Especially when real subscribers, money, access or live automations are involved.

01–10 / THE EXECUTIVE DASHBOARD

The Executive Dashboard

Copy one, replace anything in [BRACKETS], and make the AI show its work before it starts changing things.

001 / Give me the real state of this newsletter.

Analyze this publication’s performance over the last 12 months, 90 days, 30 days and 7 days. Identify meaningful changes in subscriber growth, opens, clicks, acquisition, revenue and engagement. Ignore vanity metrics. Tell me what’s getting healthier, what’s deteriorating and the three issues I should care about most.

002 / Find the inflection points.

Review our historical publication performance and identify dates or periods where subscriber growth, engagement, clicks or revenue noticeably accelerated or declined. Then investigate what changed around those periods and give me the most likely explanations.

003 / Build my Monday CEO brief.

Give me a concise executive report for this publication covering the previous 7 days: subscriber change, best and worst content, strongest acquisition sources, engagement changes, monetization signals, unusual activity and the three actions most worth taking this week.

004 / Tell me what I’m measuring that doesn’t matter.

Look at the performance data available for this publication. Separate useful decision-making metrics from vanity metrics. Tell me which numbers I should watch weekly, monthly and quarterly, and what decision each metric should influence.

005 / Compare this month to normal.

Compare the current month with our normal historical performance. Flag anything materially above or below baseline and investigate what may explain the difference.

006 / Diagnose stagnation.

Our newsletter feels stagnant. Examine subscriber growth, engagement, publishing consistency, acquisition sources and content performance. Diagnose whether the primary problem is acquisition, activation, retention, engagement, monetization or something else.

007 / Find hidden wins.

Look for statistically or strategically interesting improvements in our recent publication data that I might overlook because the headline metrics don’t make them obvious.
