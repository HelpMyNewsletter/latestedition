Field Notes / July 28, 2026

Yes, I use AI. No, I'm not hiding it.

Substack now lets readers scan eligible writing for signs of AI assistance. Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Pinterest, and LinkedIn are also making AI involvement more visible through labels and disclosures. Some are beginning to surface provenance data, too.

I'm not especially rattled by that. Teaching publishers to use AI responsibly is part of my work. What matters to me is whether a real person chose the direction, checked the claims, made the judgment calls, and approved the finished work.

Reader research reflects that distinction. People are much more comfortable when a human leads and AI assists than when AI is allowed to operate as the author.

The Transparency Shift

The platforms are making more of the production process visible.

Substack: Readers can request an AI-likelihood scan on eligible posts, Notes, replies, and comments longer than 100 words. The result is still an estimate. It can't prove who originated the idea, selected the evidence, or approved the finished work.

Meta and TikTok: Facebook, Instagram, Threads, and TikTok may add a label after a creator disclosure or when technical signals are detected. Content Credentials can travel with the file, too.

YouTube: Realistic synthetic or meaningfully altered content requires disclosure. Ordinary production assistance, including outlining, captions, titles, and idea development, generally does not.

Pinterest and LinkedIn: Labels and Content Credentials increasingly reveal how media was made, edited, or generated.

The useful distinction: A label can show that a tool participated. It can't measure originality, care, truthfulness, expertise, or responsibility.

The Ghostwriter Test

Would it feel this troubling if the helper were human?

Picture a publisher who hires a ghostwriter but still chooses the subject, defines the thesis, supplies the research, directs the artwork, creates the lead magnet, edits the draft, and approves the final send.

Most readers would see that as delegation, not deception. I would, too. Responsible AI can fill a similar production role.

The line gets crossed when automation impersonates expertise, experience, reporting, or care that no human actually supplied.

  • •The point of view still comes from a person.
  • •Evidence is still chosen and checked by the person directing the piece. Weak material gets rejected.
  • •The offer and artwork still have to serve the reader, not merely fill a slot.
  • •A human remains answerable for the final edition.

The Editorial Chain

Use AI to strengthen editorial control, not quietly replace it.

  • 01You set the premise. Decide what happened, why it matters, what you believe, and what the reader needs.
  • 02MCP retrieves the evidence. Pull from your archive, interviews, analytics, research, and project documents.
  • 03Interrogate the material with AI. Ask it to surface contradictions and missing context, then pressure-test the weakest claims.
  • 04Turn your decisions into production. Once the direction is settled, use AI to draft or edit, handle code, format the page, repurpose the idea, and prepare supporting assets.
  • 05You sign off. Verify claims, restore your voice, inspect every link, and accept responsibility.

Closing Shot

A label doesn't scare me. Careless publishing does.

Help My Newsletter already discloses AI use in its Privacy Policy and Terms of Use. I'm not pretending this work is created in some tool-free vacuum. The point is to show publishers how to use modern tools without surrendering editorial responsibility.

Use the tools, and be honest about the process. Verify what they produce. Most of all, keep a real human answerable for what reaches the reader.
