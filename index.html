FIELD NOTES // IMAGE RIGHTS

THIS WEEK'S DIAGNOSIS

You credited the photo.
Can you actually use it?

Open your last newsletter. Pick one image you didn't create.

Could you show me what allows you to publish it?

If the answer is "I credited the photographer," keep reading.

On October 28, August Image filed a copyright lawsuit against Bright Mountain Media, the publisher behind CafeMom, LittleThings, and MamasLatinas. The complaint alleges that 18 photographs were used without a license, including images allegedly copied from the internet.

Those are allegations, not a court finding. But the question they raise belongs on every publisher's checklist: where did this image come from, and what gives us the right to use it?

A credit line answers who made the image. It doesn't, by itself, answer that second question.

THE 30-SECOND READ

Find the image. Check the rights. Keep the proof.

Public-domain collections, Canva, properly licensed Wikimedia Commons files, and your own photos can all be useful sources. Each has a different check to make.

Today's job: choose one image from your next issue, verify its permitted use, and save the evidence beside the file. You'll find a permission-request template below.

THE HIDDEN MIX-UP

Credit and permission do different jobs.

Finding a photo on Google, Pinterest, Instagram, or another newsletter doesn't give you a license to republish it. Neither does linking back to the page you found it on.

You need a basis for your use: permission, a suitable license, verified public-domain status, or an applicable legal exception.

Yes, fair use exists. No, "my newsletter is free" or "I'm reporting the news" doesn't settle it. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that fair use depends on the circumstances. A simple source-and-license check is a better routine than guessing what a court would decide.

My publishing rule: If I can't explain the rights clearly, I find another image or get the question resolved before publishing.

YOUR IMAGE SOURCES

Five better places to start.

1. Public domain and CC0 collections

Try the Library of Congress Free to Use and Reuse sets, The Met's Open Access collection, and Smithsonian items marked CC0. Look for the rights statement on the exact item, not just the institution's name.

Publicly available isn't the same as public domain. And an old-looking image isn't proof. Save the item page and its rights statement. CC0 addresses copyright; privacy, publicity, or trademark rights can still matter to your use.

2. Paid stock, including Canva

Canva can be a practical source for newsletter graphics. Its license permits newsletter designs, subject to the content type and restrictions. Pro content is licensed per design; a Pro subscription issues those licenses as you use it in new designs.

Keep the design link, asset details, export date, and license record. For stock from any provider, check whether the image is cleared for your intended editorial or promotional use. Buying access doesn't mean buying unrestricted ownership.

3. The right Wikimedia Commons file

Wikimedia Commons is a useful source when you check the individual file page. Read the author, original source, and exact license. Commons itself says its licensing information isn't a warranty. Verify the record before using it.

4. Free stock with explicit license terms

Images covered by the Unsplash license can be used commercially under its terms. That copyright license does not automatically clear recognizable people, brands, or artwork shown in a photo. Use the correct license for the asset, including separate terms for paid collections.

5. Your own photos or permission-cleared images

Original photos and graphics reduce the hunt for permission. If you hire a photographer, agree in writing on the uses you need. Paying for a shoot does not automatically transfer copyright. For PR or reader-submitted images, confirm that the sender can authorize the use and ask for the required credit.

One more option: skip the photo. A useful text heading, original chart, or simple graphic can do the job without a decorative image.

THE CANVA DETAIL TO CHECK

Pro does not mean every use is covered.

Check for labels such as Editorial Use Only, Branded Content, or education restrictions. They can change what is allowed. Editorial-only content is not cleared for an ad or sponsored promotion.

There's also a detail in section 5A of Canva's current agreement: unedited Pro content in online publications has a 480,000-total-pixel limit, with specified exceptions for Canva-published websites and Canva-hosted embeds.

If you're exporting a large, unedited stock photo into beehiiv, check that clause. Don't assume a crop or a text overlay automatically resolves every restriction.

THE COMMONS CHECK

Read the letters after “CC.”

CC BY 4.0 allows commercial reuse with required attribution, a license link, and an indication of changes.

CC BY-SA 4.0 adds ShareAlike: if you adapt the image, the adaptation must use the required same or compatible license. That does not automatically mean your whole newsletter must carry that license.

If you encounter NC elsewhere, it means noncommercial. A free-to-read newsletter is not automatically noncommercial. Creative Commons says the specific use matters.

A credit line to adapt:

“[Image title]” by [creator], via [linked source page], licensed under [linked license and version]. [Describe changes, if any.]

Follow the actual file's terms and retain any required notices. Credit the creator, not merely "Wikimedia" or the person who uploaded the file. Put the credit where readers can find it in both email and the web post.

THE FIVE-MINUTE FIRST PASS

Build one image record before you send.

01 / Identify it. Save the original source URL, creator, and asset ID or filename.

02 / Name the permission. Record the license and version, the item-level public-domain statement, or the written permission. Save a dated copy of the evidence.

03 / Match the use. Email, permanent web archive, social promotion, and a sponsor's ad are different uses. Confirm the ones you need, including any edits.

04 / Add the credit. Keep the required wording and links with the image. If a designer hands you the file, ask them to hand over this record too.

05 / Test the finished issue. Check the image, credit, and links on a phone and on the web. Keep anything unresolved out of the send until it's cleared or replaced.

Your rights-log fields:

Image / creator / source / license or permission / permitted uses / required credit / evidence saved / issue date.

In beehiiv, keep a matching record outside the media gallery. A file being available to insert tells you where it's stored. It doesn't explain what you're allowed to do with it.

COPY, ADAPT, SEND

Ask for the permission you actually need.

Hi [name], I'd like to use [exact image or link] in [publication], including its email edition, permanent web archive, and [specific promotional uses]. The newsletter [has sponsors / promotes my business / charges subscribers, as applicable]. May I use it for those purposes and [describe edits]? Please confirm that you can grant these rights and share any fee, limits, and required credit. Thank you!

Replace the bracketed details and remove uses you don't need. Save the reply with the image. If the answer is vague, resolve that before the file goes into your next issue.
