THE SEND-DAY DECISION

Should you send that newsletter again?

Picture this. You’ve just sent the Black Friday issue. Someone replies, “My business partner never got it.” Your sponsor wants to know how the link is doing. You open beehiiv and the click count looks awfully quiet.

Would you hit resend? I’d want one more detail from that reader first. Which inbox was their partner checking? On November 12, “Outlook” would have changed my answer.

If Creator Spotlight sent you, welcome. We spend Tuesdays here fixing the little things that can ruin a perfectly good newsletter. You picked quite a week to pull up a chair.

Today’s triage · Before you send a second copy, find out whether the first email is late, the report is late, or the reader actually needs a correction.

01 / Two alerts, 53 minutes apart

2:58 p.m. Central, November 12. beehiiv reported a delay in click verification. Readers could still click. Raw clicks, opens, and deliveries were still counting. For a while, some clicks showed up as unverified, making the verified total look too low. Read the click incident.

3:51 p.m. Central, the same afternoon. Microsoft-hosted inboxes, including Outlook and Office 365, were delaying some incoming email. beehiiv said the mail was still queued and worked through the backlog. It marked the incident resolved on November 13. Read the delivery incident.

Imagine emailing a sponsor at 3:05 to say the ad flopped. Or sending a second copy at 4:00 to everyone who hadn’t opened. In the first case, you’d be reporting an unfinished count. In the second, some readers could get both copies over dinner.

02 / What I’d check with the clock running

  1. Open the message a reader received. Tap the main link on your phone. If the offer says Friday, does the landing page say Friday? Can you actually claim it? A working button that lands on last year’s sale is still broken.

  2. Look up the send. In beehiiv, open the published post and go to Performance. In the email view, expand Show Verified Clicks. Compare raw and verified numbers, then check the status page for a reported delay. See where the numbers live.

  3. Decide who needs what. Mail queued? Give the reader the web edition and set a time to recheck. Link broken? Fix the destination and test the link in the original email. An incorrect date or a link you can’t repair may warrant a short correction to the people affected.

What I’d tell the sponsor

“The booking link works. Click verification is running behind, so I’ll check again at 4 p.m. Central and send you the updated count.”

Only promise an update you can put on your calendar.

03 / Gmail changed the handoff, too

On November 10, Google began expanding Gemini conversation summaries in Gmail to eligible adult Workspace users with smart features enabled. The feature summarizes long email threads. Picture a reader forwarding your event to a coworker, then six people replying about who can go. Read Google’s rollout note.

Here’s a writing habit I’d take into 2027. Give the person forwarding your email one complete sentence they can lift, deadline and all. “Last chance!” only makes sense to the person who read the rest.

Try this instead. “Reserve your workshop seat by Friday at 5 p.m. Central using the registration link below.” If that line lands in a team thread on Thursday afternoon, it still does its job.

04 / Put this in the 2027 send calendar

Pick the first issue you’ll send in January. Beside its date, write down the one link readers must be able to use, who will test it from a delivered copy, and when you’ll give the sponsor a final count. If that’s all you, put your own name down.

Then run a two-minute drill. Tell the person on send duty, “A reader in Outlook says it never arrived. Our registration closes in an hour.” A good answer gives that reader the working web link immediately, checks the queue, and sets a time to follow up.
